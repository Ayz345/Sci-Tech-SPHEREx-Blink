01 · Why we built it

Anyone, anywhere
on Earth, can help
look for new worlds.

No telescope, no account, no code: only a browser.

We built a blink machine for SPHEREx. It finds every SPHEREx exposure of a spot in the sky, builds one clean frame per pass and plays them, so what moved or changed stands out. Give it any position or name; the five challenge targets open at once from stored frames.

Volunteers have already discovered asteroids, comets, stars and brown dwarfs by comparing pictures of the sky taken at different times. SPHEREx could hold the next ones.

For the challenge’s five targets, the tool read 1,949 SPHEREx exposures.

02 · How we read the archive

We take only
the rows we need.

A SPHEREx image file is about 66 MB. We read its header, work out where the target falls on the detector, and ask the archive for just those rows of the image, flag and variance layers.

We indexed the archive ourselves: the pointing and time of all 1,628,230 Level 2 images in the public bucket (to 5 October 2026), read from the public files. Any position opens without waiting for the archive’s search service.

03 · How we clean

We hold the
sky still.

Every pass and every band is drawn on one grid fixed on the sky (3″ pixels, north up), so a moving object changes place on screen and the stars do not.

We mask 20 kinds of flagged pixels, subtract the local background, and combine the exposures of a pass with a clipped, variance-weighted mean. All frames of a band share one brightness scale, so the blink does not flicker.

04 · What we found

WISE 0855,
where it moved to.

The challenge’s coordinate is where this brown dwarf was around 2010; it is now about 2.2′ west of it. We cut our own wavelength window, 4.3–5.0 µm, and it is there in all three passes (S/N 10.9, 17.2, 19.9).

Mark it once in the WISE image from 2010 and once in SPHEREx in 2026, and the tool measures its motion: 8.1 ± 0.3 ″/yr, towards the west. The published value is 8.15 ″/yr.

The source seen at short wavelengths is a fixed Gaia star, not the brown dwarf. The brown dwarf is closing on that star: 11.4″, 8.2″, then 3.8″ away. From SPHEREx alone we measure −8.8 ± 1.2 and +1.5 ± 1.2 ″/yr (published: −8.12, +0.68; Luhman & Esplin 2016).

05 · What we added

We added
the spectrum.

Click any point: its brightness against wavelength, 0.75 to 5.0 µm, measured on the single exposures, and against time.

Neighbours within 25″ are fitted together with the target, so their light is not counted as its own. Saturated points are shown as lower limits. On RW Aurigae the brightness changes between passes in all six detector bands, measured against control stars in the same images. You can also choose your own wavelengths: drag a range on the spectrum and the tool blinks only the exposures taken there; that is how we picked out WISE 0855.

06 · What we added

We added a way to
measure and report.

Mark an object in two frames and the page gives its shift, direction and rate. The server then measures it again on every single exposure and writes the positions as a table, and for Solar System objects as a draft in the Minor Planet Center’s format, checked against the official schema.

It is a draft: nothing is sent anywhere. We tested the positions on asteroid (433) Eros against JPL Horizons: 382 of 450 exposures accepted, median offset 0.12″ and 0.05″, scatter 0.17″. The observation time is not independently verified. For every point of a spectrum the tool names the flags the SPHEREx pipeline set on those pixels, such as a cosmic-ray hit, a dead pixel or the halo of a bright star.

The tool compares the passes by itself and rings what moved or changed. Given only the WISE 0855 field, with no position, it rings WISE 0855 and measures its motion at 8.7 ″/yr; it finds Epsilon Indi B and Barnard’s Star the same way, and marks RW Aurigae as changing in brightness.

It finds objects that move at least about 10″ over the time covered, and works less well in crowded fields. The rings are places to look, not detection claims: in these five fields it also rings 14 other spots that we have not confirmed.

07 · What we tested it on

We tested it on
five targets.

The five objects the challenge names, each in its own SPHEREx frames.

Gaia stars are drawn where they should be on each date; blink, difference, sum and false colour; the whole view lives in the address, ready to share or embed. A sky-context panel shows the same place in AllWISE, 2MASS or DSS2.

We added the WISE image from 2010 as the blink’s first frame, and the CatWISE catalogue as an overlay. Fields of 3′ or 7′ on this site, and 15′ when you run the server yourself; the tool tells you what the large one costs before it opens.

08 · What we do not claim

Real data,
with its limits.

  • Every SPHEREx frame, spectrum and number in the tool comes from the archive; the 2010 frame is the AllWISE image, marked as another instrument. Nothing in it is synthetic.
  • A SPHEREx pixel is 6.15″ across. Frames are drawn on a 3″ grid; the finer grid and any zoom are cosmetic and add no detail.
  • The tool claims no discovery. It makes looking easier.
  • The spacecraft model is AI-generated (3D Jutsu); it and the shapes on this page are illustrations, not data.

      About SPHEREx Blink

      We built a blink comparator for SPHEREx: it finds every SPHEREx exposure of a spot in the sky, builds one clean frame per pass on a fixed grid and plays them, so what moved or changed stands out. The five challenge targets open at once from stored frames; any other position is built on request by our server from the public archive.
